A family recording, turned into one song

What happened when I used a 40-second family recording to make a keepsake song, and what I would prepare differently.

Forty seconds of a kitchen conversation, one song, and a lesson about consent that mattered more than the audio quality.

The cleanest thirty seconds beat the full six minutes. Most of the original was clatter and overlapping voices, and the model latched onto the noise as if it were melody. Trimming first, then normalising, gave a result that needed one revision instead of five.

Six lyric lines went in, three came out recognisable. The lines that survived were the ones with a physical detail: the radio on the sill, the walk to the bus. Abstract lines about love and memory were rewritten into something generic, which is the failure mode described at Memories Song.

Tempo was the one thing worth revising. Sixty-eight beats per minute read as funeral; seventy-four read as reflective. Same lyric, same voice, completely different room.

The consent conversation came first, and it changed the project. Two people in the recording asked not to be included, so the final version uses one voice and no names. The song is shorter and better for it.
